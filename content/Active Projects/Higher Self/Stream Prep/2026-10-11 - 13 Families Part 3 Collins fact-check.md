Frame for the episode: **what the book claims vs what the record shows.** Read the claim, show the receipt, give the verdict.

**Sources:**

## The big picture in one paragraph

Springmeier opens by grading his own research on the Collins family "about at 10" on a scale of 0 to 100 (p. 27). The chapter then asserts that the Collins are "even more powerful than the Rothschilds" (p. 30), with no document behind it. Every "hard connection" for that claim comes from unnamed ex-insiders, and the only one he names and vouches for at length is **John Todd**, who gets 10 of the chapter's 21 pages (pp. 38-47). Springmeier writes, "I know of no one else who has openly talked about the Collins family" (p. 39).

The record on Todd:
- His own **witch name was "Lance Collins."** *Christianity Today* reported this in 1979, and he also used "John Todd Collins."
- His claimed Vietnam Green Beret service was **25 days overseas, in Germany, as a clerk-typist**.
- A psychiatric exam cited **"pseudologia phantastica"** (pathological lying).
- He was **convicted of rape in South Carolina in January 1988** and sentenced to 30 years.

So the chapter's star witness for "the Collins bloodline" is a man who gave himself the Collins name. The chapter even ends by asking readers to mail checks made out to Fritz Springmeier (p. 46).

The rest is a phone-book method: list notable people surnamed Collins and call each a "suspect." Many of the résumé facts are real (Fort Collins, the Collins Line, Governor John Collins, Mabel Collins, Ross Collins). None ties any of them to each other, let alone to a bloodline.

The irony, as in Part 2: the book briefly considers the one Collins with a genuinely powerful family behind him (astronaut Michael Collins: father a major general, uncle the Army Chief of Staff) and **clears him**. Its only real dynastic thread is William **Collins** Whitney, whose mother was a Collins. It runs through Skull and Bones, the Navy, the Vanderbilts and the Harrimans, and that thread is documented.

## 1. Springmeier's own score: "about at 10" out of 100

- **Claim (p. 27):** "In terms of research on the Collins family from a scale from 0 to 100, I'm about at 10. There are some hard connections that will be presented and some soft connections." He also warns: "In this Collins article, there will be some padding."
- **Verdict: Documented (it's his own words)**, and it frames everything after it. On p. 33 he admits: "Articles like this aren't as much fun to write, when I have to pad out some hard evidence with clues."
- **On air (Ego):** "He graded his own homework a 10 out of 100. Then he told us they run the planet."
- **If he pushes back:** A low score could mean the family hides well. But a claim with no evidence can't be told apart from a family that doesn't exist, and the author says he padded the chapter.

## 2. The 1955 "Grande Mother Collins" meeting

- **Claim (pp. 27-28):** A twice-yearly meeting, attended by the Rothschilds and "all the mother families," was presided over by a "Grande Mother" Collins on a rotating ebony-and-gold throne. Her sons, one of them Tom Collins, handed out "time tables." The Ark of the Covenant was "hidden in Africa." Seven children were sacrificed.
- **Verdict: Unverifiable / Unsupported.** One unnamed "ex-insider who is now a Christian" is the only source. There are no names, place, document or corroboration. In the same chapter Springmeier concedes: "Except for the testimony of eye-witnesses, there is little evidence of what happens at hierarchy meetings" (p. 30).
- **Context:** This is the same form as the 1980s "Satanic panic" ritual-abuse testimony. The chapter itself quotes an FBI report (paraphrased, p. 29) saying occult involvement in crime "usually turns out to be secondary, insignificant, or nonexistent." That matches the FBI's published conclusion of the era, Kenneth Lanning's 1992 investigator's guide. *Unverified this session: I didn't reopen Lanning's report; cite it only after checking.*
- **On air (Mind):** "One anonymous witness, no names, no place, no paper. That's not a hard connection. That's a campfire story with a throne."
- **If he pushes back:** Secret meetings are by definition hard to document. But the book calls this a "hard connection," and by its own standard it's testimony with nothing to test it against.

## 3. "Tom Collins" gunned down for talking

- **Claim (pp. 28-29):** Tom Collins left the Illuminati, toured churches warning people, and "was gunned down in a Grocery store parking lot." Covens now warn, "Remember Tom Collins."
- **Verdict: Unverified.** No date, city, or name of the Baptist church is given, and I found no news record. Note the name: a "Tom Collins" is also a well-known cocktail.
- **On air (Ego):** "A man named after a cocktail, shot in an unnamed parking lot, in an unnamed city, in an unnamed year."
- **If he pushes back:** If it happened, a shooting leaves a police report and an obituary. Give us one detail we can check and we'll check it.

## 4. Yvonne Collins ("Legena") got the SEC to investigate Jerry Falwell

- **Claim (p. 28):** Yvonne Collins of the "Grande Druid Council," occult name "Legena," got upset with Falwell and "got the Security and Exchange Commission to investigate his church."
- **Verdict: Partly (the investigation is real; who triggered it is Unsupported).**
- **Record:** The SEC really did investigate Falwell's Thomas Road Baptist Church, starting in 1972. In 1973 it charged the church with "fraud and deceit" over about **$6.5 million in unsecured church bonds**, used to take the *Old Time Gospel Hour* national. A federal court cleared the church, which then replaced its volunteer accountants with professionals. No source names any "Yvonne Collins" or any outside instigator. The "Grande Druid Council" list Springmeier relies on is John Todd's (see §11).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Road_Baptist_Church (search); https://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/jerry-falwell (search)
- **On air (Mind):** "The SEC case is real: 1973, $6.5 million in church bonds, and the church won. What's missing is any witch who called it in."
- **If he pushes back:** The real case had an obvious cause, unsecured bonds sold to small investors, so it doesn't need a hidden hand to explain it.

## 5. "The John the Baptist of the Anti-Christ": Benjamin Creme

- **Claim (p. 28):** The 1955 meeting's mystery figure "match[es] Benjamin Creme. Benjamin Creme was born in 1924." He claims that in 1959 he telepathically received a message from his Master.
- **Verdict: Partly.** Creme was born **5 December 1922** in Glasgow, not 1924, and died in 2016. He did say he was "first contacted telepathically by his Master in January 1959." He later edited *Share International* and published through the Tara Center. Nothing places him at any 1955 meeting; Springmeier's link is "the descriptions … match."
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Creme (opened)

- **If he pushes back:** Creme's New Age "Maitreya" campaign was real and public; it was full-page newspaper ads in 1982. That's the opposite of a secret.

## 11. John Todd, the chapter's star witness

- **Claims (pp. 38-47):**
  - Johnny Todd, "of the Collins family Todds," was on the Illuminati's Grand Druid Council and converted to Christ in 1972/73.
  - Springmeier rates him "around an 80" out of 100 as a source on Satanism and is "convinced he is for real."

  - His earliest release would be 2005.
  - The book asks readers to send money with checks made out to Fritz Springmeier (p. 46).
- **Verdict: False / Unsupported, and the most important finding of this fact-check.**
- **Record:**
  - **His Collins name is self-given.** *Christianity Today* (Feb. 2, 1979) heard a recording in which Todd's "witch name is Lance Collins." Wikipedia lists his aliases as "John Todd Collins" and "Lance Collins." Jack Chick's comic *Spellbound?* features a former druid named "Lance Collins" based on him. The witness for the "Collins bloodline" took the name Collins himself.
  - **Military story:** he claimed to be "a decorated Green Beret in Viet Nam." CT obtained records showing he "spent only twenty-five days overseas," in Germany, not Vietnam (service Feb. 1969 to July 1970). His discharge papers list him as a general clerk/typist.
  - **Psychiatric record:** a psychiatric exam cited "emotional instability with pseudologica phantastica."
  - **Criminal record:**
    - 1976: convicted of contributing to the delinquency of a minor (served about two months).
    - Arrested May 1987, **convicted of rape in South Carolina in January 1988**, sentenced to 30 years.
    - Released from prison in 2004 straight into the South Carolina Department of Mental Health's Behavioral Disorder Treatment Unit; died there, Nov. 10, 2007.
  - **The Thurmond frame-up:** **Unsupported.** No source ties Thurmond to the case. My searches found no record of Thurmond on BJU's board or of a 1980s resignation fight; *Unverified* either way.
  - **His prophecy:** he urged Christians to stockpile weapons and food for a Satanic takeover in 1980. It didn't happen.
- Sources: https://www.christianitytoday.com/ct/1979/february-2/legends-of-john-todd.html (opened); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Todd_(conspiracy_theorist) (opened); https://christianitytoday.com/ct/1979/february-2/editorials-bewitching-of-churches.html (search)

## 13. William Collins Whitney: the one documented "Collins" power thread

- **Claim (pp. 36-37):**
  - William Collins Whitney (Skull and Bones 1863) "of the Collins family"; two sons in Bones.
  - The "power behind" Cleveland, who "was his puppet"; a fortune "from who knows where" after the 1870s.
  - Collins blood flows into the Paynes, Vanderbilts and Harrimans.
  - Harriman and Bush financed Hitler; Thyssen wrote *I Paid Hitler*.
- **Verdict: Partly. The family facts are Documented; "puppet" and "from who knows where" are False or opinion; the Hitler line is overstated.**
- **Record:**
  - **The Collins link is real.** Whitney's mother was **Laurinda Collins**, a descendant of Plymouth governor William Bradford.
  - **Yale, Bones, sons:** Yale 1863 and Skull and Bones. Sons Harry Payne Whitney (Yale 1894) and William Payne Whitney (Yale 1898) are both listed as Bonesmen in biographical sources.
  - **Career:** "inspector of schools" (1872) is real, but he was then **New York City corporation counsel 1875-82**, fighting fraudulent claims after the Tweed Ring. He was **Secretary of the Navy 1885-89** in Cleveland's first cabinet and helped build the steel "New Navy." He helped secure Cleveland's 1892 nomination.
  - **Wealth:** not "from who knows where." It came from New York street railways (Broadway franchise, Metropolitan Street Railway), Nova Scotia coal and steel, real estate and racing; about $23 million at death.
  - **Marriages:** Cornelius Vanderbilt Whitney married Marie Norton in 1923 and divorced in 1929. She married W. Averell Harriman (Bones 1913) in 1930.
  - **Union Banking Corporation:** UBC, with Prescott Bush as a director, was seized under the Trading with the Enemy Act on Oct. 20, 1942 as a front for Fritz Thyssen's interests. The custodian's own file said it "could not be established" how much belonged to Thyssen, and the assets were returned after the war.
  - **Steel percentages:** the Vereinigte Stahlwerke figures (p. 37) are **Unverified**.
  - ***I Paid Hitler*** was published in **1941**, not the 1930s.
  - **"Hitler was of the Rothschild bloodline"** (p. 37) is an antisemitic myth with no record behind it.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Collins_Whitney (opened); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Payne_Whitney (search); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Norton_Harriman (search); https://www.theguardian.com/world/2004/sep/25/usa.secondworldwar (the 2004 Guardian investigation, seen via search-result reproductions; open before citing)

## The real Collins stories the book misses or gets wrong (strongest material)

1. **The astronaut's family.** Michael Collins was born in Rome because his father, Maj. Gen. James L. Collins, was the U.S. military attaché. His uncle "Lightning Joe" Collins was Army Chief of Staff, and three generations went to West Point. The book asked whether he had "pull" and then cleared him over one patriotic sentence.
2. **William Collins Whitney.** His mother was Laurinda Collins. He served as Secretary of the Navy and built the steel "New Navy," and his sons were Bonesmen. Through marriages the line reaches the Vanderbilts and the Harrimans. It's documented and public, through the Whitney name.
3. **The Collins Line.** A government-subsidized luxury shipping line that lost two ships in two years (Collins's own wife and children died on the *Arctic*). It folded in 1858 when Congress cut the subsidy: a cautionary tale about public money, not a hidden fortune.
4. **Governor John Collins and Trevett v. Weeden.** Rhode Island's paper-money fight (1786) produced one of the first arguments that courts can strike down laws. It's real constitutional history in a chapter that treats it as a clue.
5. **The Putnams of Salem.** The real instigators of the witch trials, which the book later absorbs into "Collins" by surname.
