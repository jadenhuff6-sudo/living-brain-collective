## The episode in four sentences

The cold open is the twist: the most famous Bundy in America isn't a Bundy. Ted was born Theodore Cowell in Vermont, "Bundy" was his stepfather's name, and that stepfather was a hospital cook, not the dentist the book claims, so the chapter's headline hook falls apart on page two (Springmeier even admits he found nothing). Then we meet the real Bundys, and they are wilder than the theory: a father who was Stimson's man on the atomic bomb, one son who ghost-wrote the official defense of Hiroshima and decided what JFK and LBJ heard, and another whose bureau drafted a war resolution two months before the Gulf of Tonkin "attack" that justified it. The book misses all of that, and instead spends its pages on MJ-12 (fake documents), a kidnapped Howard Hughes (the 1970s Gemstone File) and an "Eric Bundy" who appears in no record anywhere.

## The whiteboard thesis

Write three words on the board: **BRIEF. DRAFT. DEFEND.**

- **Brief:** decide what the president hears. McGeorge Bundy ran the national security staff for JFK and LBJ; he chose which memos, which options, which odds ("somewhere between 25% and 75%," Feb 7, 1965).
- **Draft:** write the paper before the event. A Southeast Asia war resolution existed on June 11, 1964; the Gulf of Tonkin incident came in August. NSAM 273 was first drafted Nov 21, 1963.
- **Defend:** write the story after. The official case for Hiroshima, signed by Stimson in 1947, was largely written by McGeorge from his father's memo.

**The punchline:** Part 1's Astors held power through access, information and silence. The Bundys held it through the pen, all on the record, in the State Department's own published documents. **The tell to teach:** when a book explains power with blood and Satan, it skips the paper trail that actually shows who held the pen.

## What the book missed (the strongest material)

1. **A war resolution written before the incident.** "Draft Congressional Resolution," June 11, 1964, from the Far East bureau William Bundy ran, circulated by McGeorge on June 15. LBJ shelved it, then used the idea after Tonkin in August. (Web 8)
2. **The ghost-written A-bomb defense.** Stimson's Feb 1947 *Harper's* article "The Decision to Use the Atomic Bomb" was largely written by McGeorge from Harvey's memo, on Conant's suggestion, edited by Groves. (Web 9)
3. **The Vietnam escalation memos.** Jan 27, 1965 "fork in the road" (current policy "can lead only to disastrous defeat"); Feb 7, 1965 after Pleiku, "sustained reprisal," odds "between 25% and 75%." (Web 10, Web 11)
4. **NSAM 273 drafted the day before Dallas.** First draft Nov 21, 1963; the signed version (Nov 26) widened covert action planning against the North. (Web 12)
5. **The CIA "executive action" briefing.** Richard Bissell briefed McGeorge early on an "executive action" capability, a term that included assassination (Church Committee p. 121). (Web 13)
6. **Cuban Missile Crisis, day one.** McGeorge told JFK about the missile photos at 8:45 a.m., Oct 16, 1962, after letting him sleep. (Web 14)
